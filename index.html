Crafting and Evaluating Research Designs · Alternative research design

What if the smile was never the problem?

The “Emotions and Firm Performance” study found that stores whose clerks smiled more sold slightly less. This page rebuilds those 576 stores on a computer, lets you switch everyday behaviours on and off, and shows what the original research design would have concluded next to what is actually true in the model.

The page starts with every rule off, and the original design finds nothing. Run the full model to switch the three mechanisms on one by one and watch the published number appear.

How this page works

Switch mechanisms on, measure like the original study, then compare with the truth

The puzzle

The published regression gives displayed friendliness a coefficient of −.10 on sales. Either friendliness really lowers sales, or something else creates the pattern, for example busy stores selling more while their hurried clerks stop smiling. A field survey cannot tell these apart. A simulation can, because the researcher writes the rules and therefore knows the truth.

Four steps, repeated every time you change a setting

  1. Build the stores. 576 artificial stores, each with its own customer traffic and its own friendliness norm. The two are unrelated by construction.
  2. Switch on mechanisms. The three cards below decide how customers and clerks behave.
  3. Measure like the original study. Virtual mystery shoppers visit every store, and the original regression runs on their notes.
  4. Compare with the truth. The model reruns the same stores with friendlier clerks and reports the true effect on sales.

1 · Set up

Choose which mechanisms are at work

Each card is one candidate explanation. Switch it on or off, then set how strong it is. Results update as soon as you release a control.

ReinforcementThe original study’s hypothesis

Do friendly clerks bring customers back?

A customer who was greeted, smiled at and thanked is more likely to come back. If this holds, friendliness should raise sales.

Store paceThe rival explanation

Do busy clerks stop smiling?

Clerks drop the smile when people are waiting. Busy stores sell more because more people come in, so friendliness and sales can look negatively related without friendliness doing anything.

Service timeThe cost explanation

Does smiling slow the line?

Each friendly transaction takes longer. Lines grow and hurried customers leave without buying. If this holds, friendliness genuinely costs sales.

5 · Reruns

Is the result stable, or did one run get lucky?

Who walks in when, and whether a clerk smiles, are decided by random numbers. Rerunning the same settings with 20 different seeds shows how much the coefficient moves by chance alone. If the dots cluster on one side of zero, the pattern is a real consequence of the chosen mechanisms.

6 · Findings

What the simulation answers, question by question

Recorded runs with seed 2026, 20 reruns per setting (seeds 2027 to 2046) and strength sweeps over five seeds. Press the Compare and Reruns buttons above to reproduce them on this machine.

1

Does the published −.10 show that smiling costs sales?

Not on its own. A coefficient near −.10 appears in the model only when a friendly transaction costs service time. Even then the true cost of making every store 20 points friendlier is small, between −0.6% and −2.6% of sales depending on the mix. The coefficient is in standard deviations of sales and is not an effect size.

2

Which everyday mechanism could have produced the published pattern?

No single one. Store pace alone reproduces the queue link (friendlier stores have shorter lines, −.20 against −.18) but leaves the coefficient a coin flip, negative in 9 of 20 reruns. Service time alone gives a stable negative coefficient (19 of 20) but makes friendlier stores busier, the opposite of the case. Only the mix of store pace and service time, with or without reinforcement, reproduces both numbers in 20 of 20 reruns.

3

Does the study’s own hypothesis, that friendly clerks bring customers back, show up?

Not at the default strength. Raising the return chance from 30% to 40% after a friendly visit moves the coefficient by about .01 and the true effect by about +1%. The hypothesis is not refuted; it is too weak at this strength to be visible to the original design.

4

Could the original design have told these stories apart?

No. The same cross-section fits a direct cost, a hidden driver and a chain through line length equally well. Under the chain, the line-length control removes part of the effect it is meant to isolate. Reruns with different seeds spread the coefficient by about ±.04, so a single survey can land on −.10 by chance from several of these worlds.

5

What would a field study have to measure next?

Three things the case does not report: the extra seconds a friendly transaction takes (the sweeps need about 30 s for a −.10), the share of customers who leave when more than three are waiting, and the return rate after a friendly visit. Each is measurable with a stopwatch, a door counter or loyalty-card data, and each can rule a mechanism out.

7 · Model

Rules, measurement, parameters and limits

Key terms
Mechanism
A rule about how customers or clerks behave, such as “a friendly transaction makes a customer more likely to return”. Each card controls one.
Display index
The share of observed transactions in which the clerk greeted, smiled, made eye contact and said thank you. Measured by mystery shoppers, as in the case. Also called friendliness on this page.
Line length
The largest number of customers at the counter during a mystery-shopper visit. The original study used it as a control variable, and so does this page.
Regression beta
The strength of the relationship between display index and sales after line length is taken into account, in standard deviations. Negative means friendlier stores sell less.
True effect
The change in total sales when the very same stores are rerun with a higher display norm and nothing else changed. Only a simulation can report this.
Seed and rerun
The seed fixes the random numbers, so a run can be repeated exactly. A rerun is the same settings with a different seed.
Variables: what they mean here, and why they are measured this way

Every quantity on this page is defined twice: once as the original study measured it, once as the model produces it. The model copies the study’s measurement wherever the case describes it, so that the artificial mystery shoppers see the stores the way the real ones did. Where the case is silent, the choice is stated and justified.

mechanism 1		The study’s hypothesis: friendly fronts bring customers back and so raise sales.After a friendly transaction the customer’s chance of returning within 31 hours rises from 30% by the slider value (default +10 points).The hypothesis is about patronage, so it is written as a return probability. Store pace

True effectNot observable in field data.The same 576 stores, same random draws, rerun with every store norm raised by .20; the relative change in total sales.A controlled rerun of identical stores is the switch the field never had. The .20 step is about one standard deviation of the norm.

Parameters that the case does not report are assumptions. They are listed with their values under Model and are the first things a field study should replace with measurements.

Rules, in order of execution every 10 simulated seconds
  1. Arrival. New customers arrive at random at the store's hourly traffic rate, which also varies from hour to hour. Returning customers arrive when they are due.
  2. Queue or leave. A customer who sees more than three people at the counter may leave without buying. The longer the line, the more likely.
  3. Transaction. One clerk serves one customer at a time. The clerk shows positive emotion with the store's display norm as probability, reduced by the store pace mechanism for each person waiting.
  4. Service time. A transaction takes about 30 seconds, plus the service time cost if emotion is displayed.
  5. Purchase. Each completed transaction adds one basket to store sales.
  6. Learning. The customer returns later with a base probability, raised by the reinforcement mechanism after a friendly transaction. Word of mouth is folded into this probability.
Measurement, copied from the case
  1. Each store is visited twice; one in four stores a third time.
  2. A visit lasts 4 to 12 minutes and codes up to 20 transactions, at least one.
  3. The display index is the share of coded transactions with positive display.
  4. Line length is the largest number of customers at the counter during a visit, averaged over visits.
  5. Sales are standardized across stores and regressed on the display index and line length.

The original regression had eight controls. Only line length exists in this model, so it is the only control used.

What the model leaves out, and open points

Deliberately left out

  • Display norm and store traffic are drawn independently. Any correlation between friendliness and sales comes from the three mechanisms, not from how stores are set up.
  • One clerk and one register per store. No staffing decisions, no shifts.
  • No stock levels, ownership, supervision costs, region or gender composition.
  • No reaction of clerks to being observed.

Open points for revision

  • Service times, walk-out behaviour and return rates are not reported in the case. They would need to be measured in stores.
  • The three mechanisms are my candidate explanations. Others could be added, for example clerks who become friendlier when a store is slow because they are bored.
  • A mechanism mix that reproduces the published numbers is sufficient, not proven. Several mixes may fit.
  • The model overstates how well line length predicts sales. Real sales depend on much that is left out here, such as store size, location and fuel sales.
  • A single seed can land on the published value by chance. Read patterns across the reruns, not one run.
